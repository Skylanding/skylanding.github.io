# Search Agents After May 2026: Memory, Commitment, and Whether They Are Even Searching

I have been reading search-agent papers the way other people scroll Twitter: in fragments, between rebuttals. After ACL and a summer of actually using Deep Research products, a few things stopped looking like implementation details.

The old picture was ReAct plus a search API. The new picture is closer to a control loop: what to query, what to keep, when to branch, and how to stop treating parametric memory as evidence. The last four months of community work, plus a handful of industry notes, mostly argue about those four decisions.

## Three threads that keep showing up

**1. Search is a policy, not a retriever call.**

MemSearcher (ACL 2026 Findings) is the cleanest statement of the training-side problem. Concatenating the full trajectory makes the context grow without bound; keeping a compact memory makes every turn a different optimization context. Their fix is multi-context GRPO: the trajectory-level advantage is pushed back onto every turn, so the memory writer and the search policy are trained together. That is very close to the credit-assignment headache I keep running into on multi-turn agents. If the reward only lands on the final answer, the model has no idea whether a mid-trajectory search was useful or just expensive.

ITER (Aug 2026) and the slightly earlier LRAT line make the dual claim on the retriever: human-click logs are the wrong supervision. The query an agent issues at step *t* is conditioned on the original question and on previous sub-queries. Training a dense retriever on that trajectory, with previously useful documents as redundancy negatives, gives 7–13% lifts on InfoSeek-Eval and BrowseComp-Plus across several agent backbones. I used to think “better embedding model” was the retrieval story. It is not. The retrieval story is *who is querying*.

On the training-free side, HypoSearch (Sep 2026) is the paper I keep recommending. Single-trajectory agents commit too early: they see a fork, pick a branch, and then every later tool call reinforces it. HypoSearch writes lightweight hypotheses, explores them in bounded independent branches, and compares evidence before committing. Qwen3.5-122B goes from 46.7 to 60.0 on BC-small, with fewer tool calls than five independent rollouts. This is almost the same intuition as tree grafting in multi-turn policy work: do not let the first plausible path monopolize the context.

IGPO (EMNLP 2026 Industry, deployed since May) is the unromantic sibling. Catalogs change; you cannot freeze a search policy into weights. They separate *policy guidelines* from *inventory facts*, and report a 3.17% CTR lift in a 14-day A/B. Academic papers under-discuss this. A lot of “search agents” in the lab assume a static Wikipedia dump. Production search does not.

**2. Memory is becoming structured, because traces are lying.**

ReTree (Aug 2026) treats search as an evidence tree: each node stores a bounded summary, the source-linked evidence, and a revision history. When new evidence contradicts an old claim, it walks back to the node that introduced the claim, rewrites it, and prunes the poisoned branch. Full-trajectory ReAct uses 1.27–1.51× more per-step context and loses up to 25.6 points. The interesting part is not the compression. It is that compression without provenance cannot *repair* downstream reasoning; it can only forget.

SearchAtlas (Sep 2026) is the diagnostic version of the same idea. It parses trajectories into evidential query graphs and finds process failures that correlate with wrong answers more strongly than an LLM judge looking at the raw log: fragmented support, constraints that never reach the answer, and unverified parametric knowledge sneaking in. If you only score the final string, you will train agents to be fluent verifiers.

Anthropic’s note on dynamic filtering (with Sonnet/Opus 4.6) is the industry rhyme. They run code over search hits *before* the hits enter the context window: +11% on BrowseComp and DeepsearchQA, 24% fewer input tokens. Same thesis. The bottleneck is not “more browsing.” It is what you allow the model to see.

**3. A lot of “search” is just the model checking what it already knew.**

LiveBrowseComp (May 2026) is the paper that made me distrust BrowseComp numbers. They call it Intrinsic Knowledge Dependence. Agents answer up to 44.5% of BrowseComp without tools; more than half of their queries are generated from internal hypotheses rather than retrieved leads; and if you remove the supporting evidence, they do worse than a closed-book baseline. On a 335-question set whose answers depend on facts from the previous 90 days, closed-book accuracy falls under 2%, and search-augmented scores drop 25–40 points. Rankings flip.

That is a methodological result, not a leaderboard result. If your training set and your eval share the same parametric coverage, you are rewarding verification. I would not train a search policy on BrowseComp alone anymore.

## Benchmarks I would actually use

I would split eval into four jobs, not one number.

| Job | Benchmark | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hard multi-hop on the live web | BrowseComp / BrowseComp-ZH | Still the default stress test, but contaminated by parametric knowledge |
| Retriever vs reasoner, apples-to-apples | [BrowseComp-Plus](https://texttron.github.io/BrowseComp-Plus/) (ACL 2026) | Fixed ~100K corpus. BM25 + Search-R1 is 3.86%; GPT-5 is 55.9%; GPT-5 + Qwen3-Embedding-8B is 70.1% |
| “Did it actually search?” | [LiveBrowseComp](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Forival/LiveBrowseComp) | 90-day facts, 335 questions. Kills the memory shortcut |
| Human-like deep research | DeepResearch Bench, DR-Arena, LMSYS Search Arena | Reports and pairwise prefs. DR-Arena reports Spearman 0.94 with Search Arena by building live information trees |

GAIA, WebWalkerQA, HLE, xbench-DeepSearch, and InfoSeek-Eval are still useful as cheap regression suites. I would not pick a method from any one of them.

BrowseComp-Plus is the one I wish more RL papers used. Live-web APIs make “we beat Search-R1 by 4 points” almost uninterpretable. A fixed corpus lets you say whether the gain came from the retriever, the policy, or a luckier SERP.

## What I am actually chewing on

A few notes I do not want to lose.

Outcome-only GRPO on search traces is under-specified. Search-R1++ (Feb, but the finding aged well) already reported GRPO as the *least* stable optimizer in this setting, with F1 rewards collapsing into answer avoidance unless you add action-level penalties. MemSearcher’s multi-context GRPO is trying to put credit back on turns. That is the right direction. I still have not seen a convincing token-level or span-level credit story for “this retrieved sentence is why the answer is correct.”

Early commitment is a search-specific failure mode, but it generalizes. HypoSearch’s fork analysis looks a lot like a policy that cannot explore. If the first query is slightly wrong, the rest of the trajectory is a justification machine. Branch-then-compare is a harness trick today. It does not have to stay a harness trick.

The retriever and the agent should not be trained in isolation. ITER’s ablations say the original question plus previous sub-queries are the robust query representation, and previously useful documents are the strongest negatives. That is an interface problem as much as a model problem.

Eval is now part of the method. If LiveBrowseComp rankings do not match BrowseComp, then “SOTA on BrowseComp” is a claim about memorization plus search, not about search. I would rather report a pair: one static, disentangled number (BrowseComp-Plus) and one live, time-bounded number (LiveBrowseComp or DR-Arena).

I am not sure the field has decided whether a search agent is a *reasoner that sometimes retrieves* or a *retriever that sometimes thinks*. The last four months of papers are quietly voting for the second one, then evaluating with metrics designed for the first. That mismatch is the part I keep coming back to.
